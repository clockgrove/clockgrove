# Clockgrove Visual Style

**Status:** Canonical visual direction  
**Applies to:** Clockgrove's own brand art: website and documentation illustrations, product marketing, platform demo key art, the neutral platform shell's scene treatment, icons and diagrams, including image generation for them  
**Does not apply to:** world-pack art, including AI-assisted asset creation for a pack. A world's art follows that world's style bible in its own repository, in [Assetgrove's](https://github.com/clockgrove/assetgrove) bible format (Raccoon World: [clockgrove/raccoon-world#2](https://github.com/clockgrove/raccoon-world/issues/2)).

---

## 1. Visual thesis

Clockgrove should look like a **busy illustrated storybook world that happens to be alive**.

The reference feeling is a classic bustling animal town: compact neighborhoods, anthropomorphic animals doing specific everyday tasks, charming practical buildings and vehicles, tiny visual jokes, readable signage, many simultaneous stories, and enough detail that a viewer can keep discovering things after the first glance.

The Clockgrove difference is persistence and causality.

The scene is not merely packed with cute activity. It should look as if:

- the rain started ten minutes ago;
- someone has been repairing the cart all morning;
- the kits have followed their mother from the den;
- the crow has already stolen one shiny object;
- the food stall is running low;
- the puddle is getting larger;
- a delivery is late because the path is blocked;
- any inhabitant could be selected and asked what happened.

The visual style must communicate **world state** as strongly as character charm.

---

## 2. Non-negotiable population rule

**No humans. No robots.**

Do not depict human residents, human players, humanoid androids, robot helpers, robot shopkeepers, drones with faces, mechanical AI mascots, or futuristic synthetic characters.

The inhabited world belongs to animals and other world-native beings.

A human user may be implied through a point of view, cursor, Caretaker label, letter, gift, placed object, sign, or other indirect presence, but should not appear as a person in the illustrated world.

Avoid visual shorthand that suggests an AI product rather than a living world:

- glowing circuit brains;
- humanoid robots;
- transparent holographic assistants;
- neon neural networks;
- cyberpunk streets;
- generic blue-purple technology gradients;
- floating chatbot bubbles as environmental decoration.

Technology may exist when a world calls for it, but it should be contextual, practical, and subordinate to the world rather than a symbol for “AI.”

---

## 3. Primary art-language reference

Use the **spirit of classic mid-century busy animal-town picture books** as the visual north star:

- anthropomorphic animals with simple, expressive body language;
- hand-drawn warmth;
- cheerful but not fluorescent color;
- ink-like outlines with slight organic variation;
- painted or printed-looking fills rather than slick vector gradients;
- compact architecture with exaggerated readability;
- tiny props that tell stories;
- practical vehicles and tools;
- amusing signs and storefront details;
- lots of activity without losing spatial clarity;
- scenes that reward close inspection.

The goal is not to reproduce a specific copyrighted page, character, building, or composition. Create an original Clockgrove world using these broad visual qualities.

Clockgrove should feel like a grown-up continuation of that visual language: still warm and accessible, but capable of supporting weather, ecology, persistent time, simulation, memory, and sophisticated spatial storytelling.

---

## 4. Composition: the world is the protagonist

### 4.1 Prefer environmental storytelling over portraits

A Clockgrove hero image should usually show a **place full of simultaneous activity**, not one giant character portrait.

Good compositions include:

- a neighborhood intersection with several destinations;
- an isometric yard with porch, den, cedar, feeder, path, fence, and neighboring hints;
- a market or gathering area with multiple interacting groups;
- a rainy block where different inhabitants respond differently to the same weather;
- a cutaway or elevated view showing connected spaces and routines.

Characters should occupy the world rather than float in an empty poster composition.

### 4.2 Visual density

Aim for **high narrative density, medium graphical density**.

There should be many things to notice, but shapes, paths, characters, and focal events remain easy to parse.

A useful hierarchy is:

1. clear overall place and weather;
2. 3–6 obvious activity clusters;
3. 8–20 secondary details;
4. tiny jokes, clues, and environmental history for close inspection.

Avoid wallpaper-like detail where everything competes equally.

### 4.3 Multiple micro-stories

Each broad scene should ideally contain several independent but related stories. For example:

- an inhabitant is investigating a tipped container;
- a parent is guiding young inhabitants across a wet path;
- a squirrel is repairing a wheel under a small awning;
- a crow watches a shiny spoon from a roof edge;
- a rabbit carries vegetables toward a stall;
- water is overflowing from a gutter into a puddle;
- a sign has been turned crooked by the wind;
- someone has left tiny muddy footprints toward the porch.

The viewer should be able to imagine causal links without captions.

---

## 5. Camera and spatial language

### 5.1 Preferred view

For platform illustrations that need to show spatial relationships, favor:

- isometric or lightly isometric 2.5D perspective;
- elevated three-quarter view;
- enough depth to show paths, containment, and adjacency;
- mild perspective compression rather than dramatic cinematic lenses;
- readable silhouettes at thumbnail size.

This supports the Clockgrove concept because it makes **relationships between places** visible.

### 5.2 Avoid

- ultra-wide cinematic distortion;
- shallow depth of field that hides most of the world;
- dramatic low-angle hero shots as the default;
- perfectly flat side-scroller framing;
- orthographic technical-diagram sterility;
- glossy 3D game-render aesthetics unless intentionally adapted later.

Depth should help explain the world, not make it cinematic at the expense of readability.

---

## 6. Line, shape, and rendering

### 6.1 Line

Use outlines that feel drawn, printed, or gently inked:

- clear enough to separate small forms;
- slightly imperfect rather than mathematically uniform;
- dark brown, charcoal, or softened near-black more often than pure digital black;
- thicker on major silhouettes, lighter within forms when useful.

Avoid sterile vector-icon outlines or hyper-detailed comic-book crosshatching.

### 6.2 Shapes

Shapes should be:

- rounded;
- sturdy;
- readable;
- slightly exaggerated for function;
- charming without becoming babyish.

Buildings can lean a little. Rooflines can have character. Wheels can be slightly oversized. Umbrellas, baskets, wagons, signs, windows, ladders, and doors should read instantly.

### 6.3 Texture

Favor subtle physical-media texture:

- watercolor-like variation;
- gouache-like matte areas;
- colored-pencil or printed-paper softness;
- slight pigment irregularity;
- restrained paper grain.

Texture should create warmth, not noise.

Avoid glossy plastic surfaces, ray-traced realism, metallic sci-fi sheen, and clean corporate-vector perfection.

---

## 7. Color and light

### 7.1 Palette character

Clockgrove color should feel **natural, cheerful, weather-aware, and slightly vintage**.

Use:

- moss and fern greens;
- cedar and bark browns;
- warm creams;
- muted brick and barn reds;
- mustard and ochre accents;
- rainy slate blues;
- soft teal details;
- berry, rust, and pumpkin accents;
- warm window light;
- off-white rather than bright digital white.

Avoid making every surface highly saturated.

### 7.2 World palettes

Each world owns its setting and palette. Platform illustrations should use the general color
principles above; the [reference world's style guide](https://github.com/clockgrove/raccoon-world/blob/main/docs/design/raccoon-world-ui.md)
contains its setting-specific palette.

### 7.3 Lighting

Lighting should reveal time and weather while preserving illustration readability.

Good states include:

- soft cloudy daylight;
- rainy afternoon;
- golden early morning after rain;
- dusky blue evening with warm windows;
- cozy nighttime pools of practical light.

Avoid hard photographic lighting, lens flares, bloom-heavy game lighting, and dramatic noir contrast unless a specific world event calls for it.

---

## 8. Character design

### 8.1 Animal anatomy

Characters are anthropomorphic enough to perform tasks but should remain clearly recognizable as their species.

Use:

- species-specific silhouettes;
- paws, tails, ears, beaks, muzzles, and body proportions that retain animal identity;
- upright posture when useful for tools and social scenes;
- quadrupedal or natural movement when that better suits the moment;
- expressive gestures rather than human facial anatomy pasted onto animals.

Avoid generic human bodies with animal heads.

### 8.2 Clothing

Clothing is practical, selective, and story-driven.

Good examples:

- raincoat;
- apron;
- cap;
- scarf;
- tiny backpack;
- work vest;
- satchel;
- boots when visually plausible;
- gardening gloves adapted to the species;
- messenger bag.

Characters do not all need full outfits. Fur and natural anatomy should remain a meaningful part of the design.

Clothing should communicate occupation, weather response, or personality rather than turn every animal into a costume mascot.

### 8.3 Expression

Favor readable body-language states:

- curious;
- hurried;
- suspicious;
- proud;
- distracted;
- tired;
- pleased;
- annoyed;
- focused;
- conspiratorial;
- protective;
- startled.

Avoid universal open-mouth smiles. A busy living world needs characters with different emotional states.

### 8.4 Families and relationships

When depicting parents and young animals, relationships should read through behavior:

- kits following a mother;
- an adult checking behind them;
- siblings squabbling over an object;
- food being shared;
- one young animal copying another;
- a parent demonstrating a task.

This visually reinforces Clockgrove's memory and social-learning thesis.

---

## 9. Architecture and props

### 9.1 Buildings

Buildings should feel purpose-built by or adapted for animal inhabitants while remaining grounded in recognizable architecture.

World-specific architecture belongs in each world’s style guide. For platform illustrations,
choose functional buildings, shelters, signs and paths that make the depicted activity readable.

### 9.2 Functional exaggeration

Scale can bend gently for readability.

A cart handle may fit an inhabitant better than real-world engineering would. A shop door may accommodate several species. A mailbox may sit lower. A ladder may be chunky and simple.

The rule is **storybook functionalism**: objects should look usable inside the world even when proportions are charmingly adjusted.

### 9.3 Props as state

Props should communicate what recently happened:

- open or closed umbrella;
- empty basket;
- half-eaten apple;
- muddy footprints;
- bent sign;
- spilled seed;
- missing wheel;
- wet newspaper;
- stacked firewood;
- abandoned toy wagon;
- scratched container lid;
- fresh patch on a roof;
- puddle under a leaking gutter.

Do not fill a scene with arbitrary decorative clutter. Prefer **evidence**.

---

## 10. Vehicles and machines

Practical little vehicles are strongly on-brand when they serve the world:

- hand carts;
- delivery wagons;
- bicycles and tricycles adapted for animals;
- tiny utility trucks;
- boats;
- wheelbarrows;
- pushcarts;
- garden tractors;
- improvised hauling contraptions.

Vehicles may be whimsical, but their function should remain visually obvious.

Mechanical devices are allowed. **Robots are not.**

The distinction is important: a squirrel using a hand-cranked machine or inhabitant driving a tiny delivery cart reinforces world activity; an autonomous humanoid robot changes the entire visual language into generic technology fiction.

---

## 11. Weather, time, and dynamic state

Every major Clockgrove scene should consider the current world state.

### Weather indicators

- rain streaks and splashes;
- wet fur;
- umbrellas;
- puddles;
- dripping eaves;
- wind-bent leaves;
- snow accumulation;
- fog;
- shade seeking in heat;
- drying laundry or surfaces after weather clears.

### Time indicators

- long morning shadows;
- breakfast activity;
- shop opening or closing;
- lamps turning on;
- evening food preparation;
- sleepy inhabitants;
- nocturnal species becoming active;
- stars, porch lights, and lit windows.

### Seasonal indicators

- plant stage;
- leaves;
- nesting behavior;
- food availability;
- clothing;
- decorations when appropriate;
- migration or changing wildlife activity.

Never treat weather as a screen overlay disconnected from the inhabitants. It should alter what they are doing.

---

## 12. Visualizing Clockgrove's dynamic-world concepts

For concept art, diagrams, or marketing illustrations, map platform concepts into visible world behavior rather than abstract technology symbols.

### Persistence

Show unfinished work, sleeping inhabitants, clocks, changing light, accumulated weather, deliveries waiting, food depleted, repairs in progress, or traces of earlier activity.

### Memory

Show an actor revisiting a place, carrying a familiar object, teaching a route, pointing at something remembered, keeping notes, or reacting differently because of a visible past event.

### Relationships

Show proximity, shared tasks, exchange of objects, protective behavior, avoidance, greetings, teaching, arguments, or routines.

### Event history

Show evidence in the environment: footprints, broken objects, emptied containers, repaired structures, tracks, notes, changed paths.

### Conversation

Prefer characters visibly interacting in the world or a clean product UI panel connected to a selected actor. Do not fill environmental art with floating chat bubbles.

### “Why?” / inspectability

Use subtle overlays, callouts, arrows, event cards, or highlighted evidence layered around an otherwise natural scene. The world remains primary.

### Timelines and branches

Use alternate illustrated states of the same place, branching paths, before/after panels, or ghosted variations of a world. Avoid sci-fi multiverse portals unless the product context specifically wants them.

### World authoring

Show the same neighborhood with a previewed new pond, feeder, bridge, garden, or path rendered as a coherent potential state—not code raining from the sky.

---

## 13. UI integration

Clockgrove's product UI should feel like a calm window into the illustrated world, not a themed game HUD.

Use:

- generous neutral panels;
- rounded but restrained containers;
- clear typography;
- small illustrated accents;
- semantic icons;
- event and conversation panels that do not overwhelm the scene;
- world-derived thumbnail imagery;
- readable status and evidence states.

Avoid:

- faux parchment everywhere;
- cartoon buttons for every action;
- heavy wood textures around interface chrome;
- RPG inventory framing unless a world specifically needs it;
- neon sci-fi control panels;
- overly childish typefaces.

The illustration carries the personality. The UI should support inspection and conversation with modern clarity.

## 14. Image-generation composition recipe

When generating a Clockgrove environmental illustration, specify these layers explicitly:

1. **Place:** exact type of neighborhood, yard, room, market, path, waterfront, etc.
2. **Time:** morning, rainy afternoon, dusk, night, season.
3. **World state:** what recently changed or is currently happening.
4. **Primary activity:** 1–2 clear focal stories.
5. **Secondary activity:** 3–6 smaller simultaneous stories.
6. **Character species:** varied animals with specific tasks.
7. **Environmental evidence:** footprints, depleted food, wet surfaces, broken/repaired props, signs of history.
8. **Perspective:** elevated three-quarter/isometric-like overview.
9. **Rendering:** hand-drawn storybook ink and matte painted color, subtle paper texture.
10. **Brand exclusions:** no humans, no robots, no cyberpunk, no generic AI imagery, no photorealism.

---

## 15. Reusable image-generation prompt nucleus

Use this as a base and append the specific scene request:

> Create an original Clockgrove world illustration: a warm, highly readable, busy storybook environment populated entirely by expressive anthropomorphic animals, with no humans and no robots. Use an elevated three-quarter or isometric-like view, hand-drawn ink outlines with slight organic variation, matte watercolor/gouache-like color, subtle paper texture, rounded practical architecture, small functional vehicles, signs, tools, food, gardens, paths, porches, and many tiny environmental details. The scene should contain several simultaneous everyday stories, each character actively doing something rather than posing. Make weather, time of day, recent events, object state, relationships, and cause-and-effect visibly influence the activity. Use the selected world’s own setting and palette. The image should reward close inspection and make the viewer feel that every creature, object, and place could be clicked, questioned, remembered, and followed through a persistent history. Avoid glossy 3D rendering, photorealism, cinematic depth of field, cyberpunk, neon AI motifs, humanoid anatomy with animal heads, mascot posing, and empty decorative backgrounds.

---

## 16. Scene-specific prompt pattern

For consistency, image-generation prompts should follow this structure:

```text
CLOCKGROVE SCENE

Location:
[place and world pack]

Time/weather:
[time, season, weather]

Persistent world state:
[what has happened before this frame]

Primary story:
[main visible activity]

Secondary micro-stories:
- [...]
- [...]
- [...]

Characters:
[species, relationships, clothing only where functional]

Important objects/state evidence:
[props, paths, damage, food, signs, footprints, etc.]

Composition:
elevated three-quarter / isometric-like, dense but readable

Rendering:
original busy animal-town storybook illustration, organic ink line,
matte watercolor/gouache color, subtle paper texture, cozy natural palette

Absolute exclusions:
no humans, no robots, no humanoid androids, no cyberpunk,
no holographic AI imagery, no photorealism, no glossy 3D game render,
no giant mascot portrait, no characters merely posing for the viewer
```

## 17. World-specific key art

World-specific character prompts and key-art examples belong with their world. See the
[reference world's style guide](https://github.com/clockgrove/raccoon-world/blob/main/docs/design/raccoon-world-ui.md)
for its own setting, cast and scene examples. Platform prompts should describe activity, state,
causality and history using the general recipe above.

---

## 18. Negative prompt / rejection criteria

Reject or regenerate imagery that has any of these dominant traits:

- humans or human avatars;
- robots or androids;
- animals posed like a promotional character lineup;
- giant central mascot with tiny irrelevant background;
- generic fantasy village without dynamic world evidence;
- cyberpunk/neon technology language;
- glossy Pixar-like 3D character rendering as the default visual identity;
- photorealistic wildlife;
- anime character styling;
- empty scenes with no meaningful activity;
- overloaded scenes with no readable focal hierarchy;
- chat bubbles covering the environment;
- random clutter that does not imply function or history;
- every character smiling at the viewer;
- hyper-perfect vector geometry;
- sterile corporate illustration;
- technology symbols standing in for behavior.

---

## 19. Final visual test

Before accepting an image, ask:

1. Can I tell **where** this is?
2. Can I tell **what time/weather state** the world is in?
3. Are several inhabitants doing **specific things**?
4. Does the environment show evidence of **something that happened earlier**?
5. Do characters have different motives or emotional states rather than posing together?
6. Are objects and buildings functional rather than decorative?
7. Does the image reward zooming in and discovering another story?
8. Could I imagine selecting any important character or object and asking “what are you doing?” or “why?”
9. Does this feel like a persistent living world rather than a cute illustration of animals?
10. Are there absolutely **no humans and no robots**?

If most of those answers are yes, the image is probably Clockgrove.
